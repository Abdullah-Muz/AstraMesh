## 2. Analysis columns
- **problem**: Spacecraft swarms face communication delays with ground operators (minutes to hours in deep space). DSA aims to let swarms make decisions and adapt "with less human intervention" through shared autonomy. (Intro; "A Helping Hand in Orbit")
- **agent_info**: DSA "provides the swarm with a task list". It shares each spacecraft's distinct perspective, "what it can observe, what its priorities are", and integrates them into "the best plan of action for the whole swarm". The page does not say what exact data is exchanged. (Intro paragraph beginning "The DSA software")
- **networking_assumptions**: The photo caption mentions efficient task allocation "using ad-hoc networking". No link, delay, loss or topology details are given for Starling. (Lead photo caption)
- **physics**: Starling flight data came from four spacecraft measuring "electron content of plasma between each spacecraft and GPS satellites". The lunar PNT tests used ground flight computers simulating swarms "at high and low lunar orbits". Dynamics fidelity is not stated in the text. ("Sharing the Workload"; "Simulated Lunar Swarming")
- **fault_model**: There is one qualitative claim: "if one swarm member was unable to accomplish its work, the other three swarm members could react and complete the mission's goals". The page does not say whether this was a demonstrated failure or a design property. Elsewhere it says the PLEXIL-based extension could "correct issues". ("Sharing the Workload"; "A Helping Hand in Orbit")
- **safety_model**: not stated in the text, beyond PLEXIL being described as "designed for reliable and flexible automation". No safe-mode or protection logic is described.
- **method**: "decision trees and mathematical models" decide follow-on actions and responses to changes. The swarm decided what to observe "without pre-programmed instructions". The Starling 1.5+ extension adds PLEXIL (Plan Execution Interchange Language) for autonomous maintenance, issue correction and software-update distribution. ("The DSA software..." paragraph; "A Helping Hand in Orbit")
- **baselines**: not stated in the text. The page compares only qualitatively with operators instructing each satellite individually ("measurements that could have been missed").
- **metrics**: not stated in the text. The milestone list says "DSA experiments onboard Starling reach the necessary criteria for success" (March 2024), but the criteria are not given. (Milestones)
- **scale**: Flight used 4 spacecraft (Starling, operating since July 2023). The ground testbed is captioned "test racks containing 100 spacecraft computers". The lunar PNT simulation ran "nearly one hundred tests over two years" with swarms "of different sizes", with larger swarms planned from 2026. Exact swarm sizes, task counts and runtimes are not stated in the text. (Caption; "Sharing the Workload"; "Simulated Lunar Swarming")
- **key_limitations**: This is an outreach article. It has no algorithm specification, quantitative results, baselines or failure evidence, and its "firsts" are asserted rather than evidenced. The fault-tolerance statement is not clearly separated into demonstrated versus designed capability. (Whole page)
- **AstraMesh_overlap**: The page repeats NASA's claimed Starling 1.0 firsts, including "the first fully distributed autonomous operation of multiple spacecraft", distributed reactive operations, and distributed onboard planning. It also describes DSA splitting ionospheric GPS-TEC observation work across four spacecraft. AstraMesh therefore cannot claim primacy for decentralized multi-CubeSat TEC observation allocation, or for peer takeover of a failed member's work, which DSA claims at least as a property. DSA has also already run swarm scaling studies on flight-computer testbeds of roughly 100 nodes, though for a lunar PNT scenario. Any AstraMesh scalability claim should therefore be cautious and cite this work. ("Sharing the Workload"; "Simulated Lunar Swarming")
