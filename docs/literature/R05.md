## 2. Analysis columns

**problem:** which space-weather mitigation decisions exist for power, satellite and aviation operators (systematic review), which were taken during solar cycle 25 including the May and October 2024 storms, and how practice compares with the literature (§1).

**method:** literature review (Figs. 1–3); then an anonymous online survey and interviews: 303 operators contacted (50 power, 227 satellite, 26 aviation), 55 responses (18 %), 33 interviews (abstract, §3).

**findings for satellites (§4.2, Figs. 6–7):**
- Long-term measures dominate: shielding evaluation (61 %), radiation modelling in design (55 %), onboard redundancy (48 %); "it is all about design" (Id18).
- Short-term protective actions were rare: two operators moved at-risk satellites, one placed satellites into safe mode (10 %). One operator avoided safe mode "citing that the loss of services would outweigh the risk of damage, and that the customers almost always opted for continued service" (p. 19).
- During events, most continued operations and monitored; reported impacts include bus or payload resets, GPS issues, scintillation, loss of energy in orbit and one loss of satellites (Fig. 7).
- "Some operators discussed that once on mission, operators manage the system as a whole and not individual maneuvers" (p. 25).

**cross-sector conclusion:** operators enacted 79.2 % of the mitigation options found in the literature, mostly the low-cost ones, and accept risk to avoid service loss; operators asked for more accurate, localized warnings; "future research needs to properly evaluate the cost-benefit ratios ... using quantitative risk frameworks" (§6).

**key_limitations:** small, self-selected sample (only 8 countries represented); self-reported; percentages' bases are not always stated; preprint.

**AstraMesh_overlap:**
- Real-world support for the G3 trade-off: operators weigh service lost to protection against hazard, and mostly choose continuity. A protection policy that only minimises exposure would not match practice; the Phase 4 finding that false protection is the dominant cost (S11) is consistent with it.
- Support for per-spacecraft (localized) risk and for decisions made "as a whole", i.e. coordination across the fleet.
- It states the open need (quantitative cost-benefit) without providing a method.
